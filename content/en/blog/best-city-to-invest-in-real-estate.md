Finding the best city to invest in real estate means weighing rental yield, market tension and capital gain potential against each other. Some mid-sized cities post double-digit gross yields, while major cities reassure investors through liquidity and structural demand. This guide covers the criteria behind a profitable city, a ranking of cities to watch in 2026, and the pitfalls to avoid before signing a purchase agreement.

The gradual normalization of mortgage rates in 2026 is giving investors more room to maneuver, after two years of shrinking real estate purchasing power. This relief benefits mid-sized cities first, where the entry ticket stays lower than in major cities, and where a high rental yield offsets a still-moderate borrowing capacity.

## What Makes a City Profitable for Real Estate Investment?

A profitable city for rental investment combines several signals that, taken in isolation, never guarantee a project's profitability.

- **Purchase price per square meter**: the lower it is relative to achievable rent, the higher the gross yield. This is the primary factor separating mid-sized cities from major metropolitan areas.
- **Rental market tension**: a tight market, where demand outstrips available housing supply, reduces the risk of vacancy between tenants.
- **Student and young professional demand**: a university city or one with a diversified employment base guarantees a steady flow of new tenants.
- **Demographic trend**: a growing or stable population supports rents and property values over the long run.
- **Infrastructure projects**: a new transit line or a developing economic hub can drive up a neighborhood's value before the market fully prices it in.

None of these criteria works alone: a city with very low prices but declining population is no safer than an expensive, oversupplied metropolitan market. The right approach is to cross-check at least three of these indicators before selecting a city, rather than relying on a single average yield figure highlighted in a comparison.

## Calculating Gross and Net Rental Yield Before Buying

Before choosing a city, it helps to know how to calculate expected profitability yourself rather than relying solely on market averages.

**Gross yield** is calculated by dividing annual rent by the total purchase price (fees and renovation included), multiplied by 100. For a flat bought at 120,000 euros and rented at 600 euros per month: (600 x 12) / 120,000 x 100 = **6% gross**.

**Net yield** subtracts non-recoverable charges (co-ownership fees, property tax, insurance, rental management fees) and factors in the impact of the chosen tax regime. It typically represents 60-80% of gross yield depending on the tax regime and the property's charge level.

For a first project, a detailed look at [rental investment for beginners](/en/blog/invest-rental-real-estate-beginner/) helps set a realistic yield target before comparing cities, rather than focusing solely on the average figure shown in a ranking.

## Rental Tension and Student Demand: Signals to Watch

Rental tension measures the ratio between the number of prospective tenants and the number of available properties in a given market. A city under strong tension sharply limits vacancy risk, one of the main factors that erode an investment's actual profitability.

Several signals help assess this tension level before buying:

- **Student population and its trend**: a university city with a growing student base guarantees recurring demand for small units.
- **Housing vacancy rate**: published by local housing observatories, a vacancy rate below 6-7% signals a tight market.
- **Population and its trajectory**: steady demographic growth supports rental demand and prices over 10-15 years.
- **Average time to rent a property**: on the ground, a flat rented out in under two weeks confirms the tension observed in the statistics.

In mid-sized cities with the highest gross yields, rental tension can be more uneven from one neighborhood to another: precise street-level scouting remains essential before signing.

## Older vs New-Build: Rental Management and LMNP

The choice between an older property and a new-build directly affects yield, taxation and the time devoted to rental management.

**Older properties** offer a purchase price 15-25% lower than new-build for an equivalent size, with a wider choice of locations, often in city centers. In exchange, renovation work is frequently needed, and the energy performance rating should be checked before purchase to anticipate any renovation obligations.

**New-build** reduces notary fees (2-3% versus 7-8% for older properties) and short-term maintenance costs, but typically comes with a gross yield one to two points lower and a location more frequently on the outskirts.

On the tax side, the Non-professional Furnished Rental (LMNP) status at réel applies to both configurations and allows depreciating the property over 20-30 years, neutralizing much of the rental income for tax purposes during that period. Rental management delegated to an agency generally costs 7-10% of collected rent, a cost to factor into the net yield calculation. The available regimes are detailed in this overview of [real estate investment taxation](/en/blog/real-estate-investment-taxation/), useful for weighing LMNP, unfurnished regimes and tax incentive schemes according to individual tax brackets.

## Mistakes to Avoid for a Successful Rental Investment

A poorly prepared project can turn an apparently profitable city into a disappointing investment. The most common mistakes to anticipate:

- **Relying solely on the city-wide average**: within the same city, yield can vary by a factor of two depending on the neighborhood. Always check actual rents on the specific street targeted, not just the city-wide average.
- **Underestimating vacancy**: even in a tight city, one month of vacancy represents 8% in lost rent over the year. Always budget for it in the net yield calculation.
- **Neglecting financing**: a poorly negotiated mortgage rate can cut net profitability by several points, regardless of the quality of the chosen city. Comparing [mortgage financing](/en/blog/home-loan-financing/) options before committing remains a decisive step.
- **Forgetting borrower's insurance**: its cost can represent up to 30% of the total cost of the loan over its term. Insurance delegation, allowed since the Lemoine law, can generate substantial savings; the choice of contract deserves attention via this [borrower's insurance guide](/en/blog/borrower-insurance-guide/).
- **Buying without visiting the neighborhood by day and by night**: rental tension statistics never replace an on-the-ground visit to assess safety, nearby amenities and the actual condition of neighboring buildings.
